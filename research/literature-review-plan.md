# Literature Review Plan — RingoStrike

## Working Paper Direction

**From Habit Theory to Adaptive Micro-Missions: Designing RingoStrike as an Emotionally Supportive Habit Companion**

## Purpose of This File

This file defines the research plan for the literature review.

It does **not** write the literature review yet.

The purpose is to identify what research areas must be studied, what evidence is needed, what claims require support, and how the literature should later be connected to RingoStrike features.

The current paper should be treated as:

> An evidence-informed design framework and early product case study, not a validated habit-change intervention.

---

# 1. Purpose of the Literature Review

The literature review should help answer the main research question:

> How can an emotionally supportive digital companion adapt daily micro-missions, feedback, reminders, and rewards based on user behavior to support sustainable habit-building experiences?

The literature review has five main purposes:

1. **Ground the paper in existing research**

   * Habit formation
   * Behavior change
   * Motivation
   * Adaptive interventions
   * Gamification
   * Digital companions
   * Ethical persuasive design

2. **Support the design logic behind RingoStrike**

   * Why small missions may reduce friction
   * Why adaptive mission intensity may be useful
   * Why reminders and feedback need to be supportive
   * Why streaks and rewards can help engagement but may also create pressure

3. **Prevent unsupported claims**

   * The paper must not claim that RingoStrike has proven long-term habit change.
   * The literature can support design decisions, but it cannot replace product data or user studies.

4. **Build a feature-to-theory map**

   * Each major RingoStrike feature should be connected to relevant research concepts.
   * Each feature should also include risks, limitations, and evidence still needed.

5. **Prepare for a future user study**

   * The literature review should help define what should be measured in a formative study.
   * The first study should focus on clarity, perceived support, friction, motivation, and usability, not long-term habit formation.

# 3. Source Acceptance Criteria

## Accept Strong Sources

Prioritize:

1. **Peer-reviewed journal articles**

   * Habit formation
   * Behavior change
   * HCI
   * Digital health
   * Psychology
   * Motivation
   * Human-computer interaction

2. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses**

   * Especially for behavior change techniques, gamification, digital interventions, and reminders.

3. **Foundational theory papers and books**

   * Habit theory
   * Self-Determination Theory
   * Persuasive technology
   * Behavior change frameworks

4. **Conference papers from respected venues**

   * CHI
   * CSCW
   * DIS
   * UbiComp
   * MobileHCI
   * JMIR-related venues
   * ACM and IEEE venues where relevant

5. **Validated frameworks and taxonomies**

   * Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy
   * Self-Determination Theory
   * Just-in-Time Adaptive Intervention frameworks
   * Persuasive systems design models

6. **Empirical studies**

   * User studies
   * Pilot studies
   * Field trials
   * App engagement studies
   * Digital coach or chatbot studies

7. **High-quality design and HCI papers**

   * Especially papers that connect theory to product or interaction design.

---

## Accept With Caution

Use carefully:

1. **Industry reports**

   * Useful for market context or app engagement trends.
   * Not strong enough for psychological or behavioral claims.

2. **Blog posts by credible researchers**

   * Useful for explanation.
   * Not enough as primary evidence.

3. **Product case studies**

   * Useful for design comparison.
   * Not enough to prove behavioral effectiveness.

4. **Popular books**

   * Useful for accessible framing.
   * Must not replace peer-reviewed evidence.

5. **AI-generated summaries**

   * Useful only for orientation.
   * Must never be cited as evidence.

---

## Reject Weak Sources

Avoid using:

1. **Random blogs with no sources**
2. **Motivational content with no scientific basis**
3. **Marketing pages from habit apps**
4. **Fake citation lists**
5. **AI-generated references that cannot be verified**
6. **Unsourced Medium posts**
7. **YouTube videos as scientific evidence**
8. **Influencer claims about productivity**
9. **Overconfident claims from startup blogs**
10. **Any source that claims universal behavior-change results without data**

# 4. Recommended Research Order

The research should be done in this order.

Start with habit formation because it defines the foundation of the paper.

Goal:

* Understand what habits are.
* Understand repetition, cues, automaticity, and habit strength.

Create a literature matrix file later, for example:

`research/literature-matrix.md`

or

`research/literature-matrix.csv`

Recommended columns:

| Column Name                     | Purpose                                                                   |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `ID`                            | Short source ID, such as H01, BCT03, JITAI02                              |
| `Full Citation`                 | Complete APA-style citation                                               |
| `Source Type`                   | Journal article, conference paper, book, review, thesis, report           |
| `Research Area`                 | Habit, BCT, JITAI, SDT, gamification, companion, reminders, UX, ethics    |
| `Research Question Supported`   | RQ1, RQ2, RQ3, or multiple                                                |
| `Key Concept`                   | Automaticity, autonomy, prompts, rewards, emotional support, etc.         |
| `Main Finding`                  | Short summary of the source’s main relevant finding                       |
| `Method`                        | Review, experiment, survey, field study, interview, theoretical framework |
| `Sample / Context`              | Who or what was studied                                                   |
| `Relevance to RingoStrike`      | How this source connects to a RingoStrike feature                         |
| `Supported RingoStrike Feature` | Tiny missions, Ringo messages, reminders, streaks, etc.                   |
| `Claim Supported`               | Which claim from `claims-to-support.md` this source may support           |
| `Strength of Evidence`          | Strong, moderate, weak, theoretical, background only                      |
| `Limitations`                   | What this source does not prove                                           |
| `Useful Quote or Paraphrase`    | Short note, not a long copied quote                                       |
| `Citation Status`               | Found, downloaded, read, summarized, added to paper                       |
| `Notes`                         | Extra comments                                                            |

Important:

Do not add a source to the paper just because it sounds useful.

Only add it if:

* It has been verified.
* It has been read or at least carefully reviewed.
* Its relevance to RingoStrike is clear.
* Its limitations are understood.

* “Prior work indicates…”
* “This may support…”

* “Users will definitely…”

* “The companion increases motivation…”

# 10. Warning Section: Common Mistakes, Weak Claims, and Fake Academic Confidence

## Mistake 1 — Treating design logic as scientific proof

A feature can be inspired by research without being proven effective.

Example weak claim:

> Tiny missions help users build habits.

Better claim:

> Tiny missions are designed to reduce task friction, a design rationale that should be evaluated through user testing and behavioral logs.

---

## Mistake 2 — Using famous theories without connecting them to features

Do not mention Self-Determination Theory, habit theory, or gamification just to sound academic.

Each theory must connect to a specific product decision.

Example:

* Autonomy → optional bonus missions and non-shaming reminders
* Competence → clear progress feedback and achievable tiny missions
* Relatedness → emotionally supportive Ringo messages

---

## Mistake 3 — Overstating AI personalization

If the system is currently rule-based, say rule-based.

Do not call the system AI-personalized unless it actually uses AI to adapt messages or missions based on user behavior.

Better wording:

> The current framework supports behavior-aware adaptation and may later be extended with AI-assisted personalization.

---

## Mistake 4 — Confusing engagement with habit formation

High app usage does not automatically mean habit formation.

Possible engagement metrics:

* Return rate
* Mission completion
* Reminder response
* Tiny mission use

Possible habit-related evidence would require more careful measurement:

* Repeated behavior over time
* Stable context
* Automaticity
* Habit strength
* Maintenance after reminders are removed

---

## Mistake 5 — Ignoring negative effects of gamification

Streaks and rewards can motivate some users, but they may also create guilt, pressure, or failure anxiety.

The literature review must include both positive and negative perspectives.

---

## Mistake 6 — Treating emotional support as harmless by default

A companion character can feel caring, but it can also become manipulative, annoying, overly attached, or emotionally misleading.

Ringo’s design should avoid:

* Shame
* Guilt
* Fake intimacy
* Overpromising
* Dependency
* Medical or therapeutic claims

---

## Mistake 7 — Using weak sources because they sound good

Do not build the paper on productivity blogs, startup marketing, or AI-generated citations.

Every important claim needs credible sources.

---

## Mistake 8 — Writing like the app is already validated

The paper is not ready to claim scientific effectiveness.

Current correct framing:

> Evidence-informed design framework and early case study.

Future possible framing after user data:

> Formative user study.

Future possible framing after longer data:

> Empirical evaluation.

---

## Mistake 9 — Ignoring cultural and language context

RingoStrike may be tested first with a small, culturally specific group.

Do not generalize findings to all users, countries, or habit-building contexts.

---

## Mistake 10 — Hiding limitations

The limitations section will make the paper stronger, not weaker.

Important limitations to state clearly:

* No long-term habit evidence yet
* Small or no user sample
* No controlled comparison
* No validated habit-strength measurement yet
* Possible novelty effect
* Companion effect not isolated
* Gamification effects may vary by user
* Rule-based adaptation is not the same as AI personalization
* User feedback does not prove behavioral effectiveness
