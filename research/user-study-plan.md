# User Study Plan — RingoStrike

## File purpose

This file defines a realistic first user study plan for RingoStrike.

The study is designed to evaluate early user experience, not long-term behavior change.

RingoStrike should currently be studied as:

> A formative UX/product research case study of an evidence-informed habit-building prototype.

This study should focus on:

* clarity
* usability
* perceived emotional support
* perceived friction
* tiny mission usefulness
* guided flow
* Ringo companion experience
* reminders
* rewards
* pressure or guilt risks

This study must not claim that RingoStrike improves wellbeing, productivity, discipline, mental health, or long-term habit formation.

---

# 1. Purpose of the Study

The purpose of this study is to understand how early users experience RingoStrike’s habit-building flow, especially whether the app feels clear, usable, supportive, and low-friction.

The study should evaluate whether users understand:

* what RingoStrike is
* what Ringo does
* how paths, challenges, and missions relate to each other
* what today’s mission is
* how tiny missions work
* how “Make it smaller” works
* how “I’m too tired” works
* how reminders feel
* how rewards and streaks feel
* whether the app feels supportive or pressuring

The study should help improve the product before making stronger claims in the paper.

The most important goal is not to prove that RingoStrike works.

The most important goal is to identify:

* what users understand
* where they get confused
* what feels motivating
* what feels annoying
* what feels emotionally supportive
* what creates pressure or guilt
* what needs to be simplified before a larger study

---

# 2. What This Study Can and Cannot Prove

## What this study can evaluate

This study can evaluate:

* whether users understand the core flow
* whether users can complete key tasks
* whether users find tiny missions useful
* whether Ringo feels supportive, annoying, childish, helpful, or unnecessary
* whether reward moments feel satisfying or excessive
* whether reminders feel caring or intrusive
* whether users feel pressure or guilt from streaks, reminders, or Ringo messages
* whether users know what to do next
* whether the app feels simple enough for early use
* whether behavior logs show basic engagement patterns during a short test

## What this study cannot prove

This study cannot prove:

* that RingoStrike forms long-term habits
* that RingoStrike improves wellbeing
* that RingoStrike improves productivity
* that RingoStrike improves mental health
* that RingoStrike improves discipline
* that tiny missions increase long-term retention
* that Ringo increases motivation
* that reminders are more effective than normal reminders
* that rewards improve habit formation
* that adaptive missions are better than static missions
* that the app works for all users

## Correct framing

This study should be described as:

> A formative UX study.

or:

> A short formative pilot study focused on usability, perceived support, friction, and early engagement.

It should not be described as:

> A clinical study.

> A randomized controlled trial.

> A validated behavior-change intervention.

> Proof of habit formation.

---

# 3. Research Questions

## Main research question

How do early users experience RingoStrike’s companion-led micro-mission system in terms of clarity, usability, perceived support, friction, reminders, rewards, and pressure?

## Sub-questions

### RQ1 — Clarity

Do users understand the relationship between paths, challenges, daily missions, tiny missions, bonus missions, rewards, and streaks?

### RQ2 — Usability

Can users complete the core flow without help?

Core flow:

1. Start the app
2. Understand Ringo’s guidance
3. Select or enter a path/challenge
4. Find today’s mission
5. Complete a mission
6. View reward feedback
7. Understand what to do next

### RQ3 — Tiny mission usefulness

Do users understand tiny missions, and do they perceive them as useful when tired, overwhelmed, or low on motivation?

### RQ4 — Companion experience

Do users perceive Ringo as supportive, clear, warm, and helpful, or do they perceive Ringo as annoying, childish, manipulative, fake, or unnecessary?

### RQ5 — Friction and decision burden

Does the guided flow reduce confusion, or do paths, challenges, missions, rewards, and companion messages make the app feel too complex?

### RQ6 — Reminders

Do users perceive reminders as helpful and caring, or intrusive, annoying, guilt-inducing, or easy to ignore?

### RQ7 — Rewards and streaks

Do users understand rewards, XP, streaks, and step-by-step reward moments? Do these features feel satisfying, excessive, pressuring, or unclear?

### RQ8 — Pressure and guilt risk

Do any parts of the app make users feel judged, guilty, pressured, or emotionally manipulated?

---

# 4. Study Type

This research should have two possible versions.

## Version A — Small usability test

A moderated usability test with 5–8 participants.

This is the best first study if the product is still changing quickly.

It should focus on:

* first impression
* clarity
* task completion
* usability problems
* Ringo’s tone
* tiny mission understanding
* reward moment reactions
* reminder copy reactions
* pressure or guilt risks

## Version B — 7–14 day formative pilot

A short pilot with 10–20 users using RingoStrike in real life for 7–14 days.

This is better after the core flow is stable enough for daily use.

It should focus on:

* short-term engagement
* mission completion behavior
* skip behavior
* remind-later behavior
* tiny mission usage
* reminder response
* reward experience over repeated use
* perceived support over time
* friction and drop-off patterns

Version B is stronger than Version A, but it still cannot prove long-term habit formation.

---

# 5. Participants

## Version A participants

Recommended sample:

* 5–8 participants

This is enough for an early usability test because the goal is to find major problems, not to generalize to all users.

Participants can be:

* friends
* early testers
* design peers
* people interested in self-improvement apps
* people who have used habit apps before
* people who have tried and abandoned habit apps

However, if participants are friends or close contacts, the paper must say this honestly.

Use wording such as:

> Participants were recruited through convenience sampling.

Do not pretend this is a representative sample.

## Version B participants

Recommended sample:

* 10–20 users
* 7–14 days of use

This is enough for a practical pilot, but not enough for strong scientific claims.

Participants should include a small mix of:

* people who already use habit/productivity apps
* people who want to build routines but struggle with consistency
* people who like companion-style or gamified apps
* people who are skeptical of habit apps

A mixed group is better than only testing with people who already love the idea.

---

# 6. Recruitment Criteria

## Inclusion criteria

Participants should:

* be 18 years or older
* be able to use a web app or mobile web app
* understand that RingoStrike is not a medical or therapy product
* be interested in testing an early self-improvement/habit app
* be willing to give honest feedback
* be willing to have basic app behavior logged during the study
* be comfortable answering short survey and interview questions

## Recommended participant profiles

Try to include people from at least three of these groups:

1. People who currently use habit apps.
2. People who tried habit apps and stopped.
3. People who want better routines but feel inconsistent.
4. People who like gamified apps.
5. People who dislike gamified productivity apps.
6. People who respond positively to cute or companion-based interfaces.
7. People who may find companion-based interfaces childish or annoying.

This mix will give more useful feedback than only testing with supportive friends.

## Exclusion criteria

Do not recruit participants who:

* are under 18
* are expecting mental health support
* are in acute emotional crisis
* want the app to treat anxiety, depression, ADHD, addiction, or other clinical conditions
* cannot give informed consent
* cannot safely receive reminders or notifications
* are directly involved in designing RingoStrike

## Important recruitment warning

Do not market the study as:

> Test an app that will improve your life.

Better recruitment wording:

> I’m testing an early habit-building app prototype called RingoStrike. The study focuses on usability, clarity, reminders, rewards, and how the companion character feels. It is not a therapy app and it does not claim to improve mental health or long-term habits.

# 7. Study Procedure

## Version A — Small usability test procedure

### Recommended format

* Moderated remote or in-person usability session
* 45–60 minutes per participant
* Screen recording if the participant agrees
* Think-aloud method
* Short post-test survey
* Short interview

### Session structure

| Step                      |      Duration | Activity                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | ------------: | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Consent and introduction  |     5 minutes | Explain study purpose, data use, and limits                                |
| First impression          |     5 minutes | Ask participant to open the app and describe what they think it does       |
| Task-based usability test | 20–30 minutes | Participant completes key tasks while thinking aloud                       |
| Post-test survey          |   5–8 minutes | Participant rates clarity, support, friction, pressure, rewards, reminders |
| Interview                 | 10–15 minutes | Ask follow-up questions about confusion, support, and risks                |

### Moderator instructions

The moderator should not explain the app too early.

Let the participant try first.

Only help if the participant is completely stuck.

Record where users hesitate, misclick, misunderstand, or ask questions.

Important observation points:

* Do they understand what Ringo is?
* Do they understand what the app wants them to do?
* Do they know what today’s mission is?
* Do they understand tiny missions?
* Do they understand “Make it smaller”?
* Do they understand “I’m too tired”?
* Do rewards feel clear?
* Does Ringo feel helpful or distracting?
* Does anything feel pressuring?

---

## Version B — 7–14 day pilot procedure

### Recommended format

* 10–20 users
* 7–14 days
* Real-world app use
* Behavior logs
* Short daily check-ins
* End-of-study survey
* Optional follow-up interviews

### Pilot structure

| Phase                |         Duration | Activity                                                                    |
| -------------------- | ---------------: | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Setup and consent    |            Day 0 | Explain the study, consent, reminders, and data collection                  |
| Onboarding           |            Day 0 | User starts app, chooses path/challenge, completes or reviews first mission |
| Daily use            | Days 1–7 or 1–14 | User receives missions, reminders, rewards, and Ringo messages              |
| Daily micro-feedback |     1 minute/day | User answers 2–4 short questions                                            |
| Exit survey          |        Final day | User rates clarity, support, friction, reminders, rewards, pressure         |
| Interviews           |      After pilot | Interview 3–5 users or all users if possible                                |

### Recommended daily participant instruction

Participants should be told:

> Use RingoStrike naturally. You do not need to complete every mission. If you feel tired, confused, annoyed, or unmotivated, use the app honestly. Skipping, postponing, or choosing a tiny mission is useful feedback for this study.

This matters because the study needs real behavior, not forced compliance.

# 8. Tasks for Participants

## Version A tasks

Participants should complete these tasks during the moderated session.

### Task 1 — First impression

Open RingoStrike and describe what you think this app is for.

Observe:

* Can the user explain the product?
* Do they understand Ringo’s role?
* Is the first screen clear?

### Task 2 — Understand the structure

Ask the participant:

> Please explain what you think paths, challenges, and missions mean.

Observe:

* Are the terms clear?
* Is the hierarchy confusing?
* Does the user know where to start?

### Task 3 — Start or choose a path

Ask the participant to choose a path or enter the recommended flow.

Observe:

* Do they know what to click?
* Do they feel overwhelmed by choices?
* Does Ringo guide them clearly?

### Task 4 — Find today’s mission

Ask:

> What does the app want you to do today?

Observe:

* Can they identify the main mission?
* Are tiny/bonus/other missions distracting?
* Is the primary action clear?

### Task 5 — Complete a normal mission

Ask the participant to complete a mission.

Observe:

* Is the Done button clear?
* Does the completion flow feel satisfying?
* Is the reward moment understandable?

### Task 6 — Use “Make it smaller”

Ask:

> Imagine this mission feels too much today. What would you do?

Observe:

* Does the user find “Make it smaller”?
* Do they understand what it means?
* Does the smaller mission feel useful?

### Task 7 — Use “I’m too tired”

Ask:

> Imagine you are tired and do not want to do the full mission. What would you press?

Observe:

* Does the user feel comfortable pressing it?
* Does the response feel supportive or awkward?
* Does it reduce pressure?

### Task 8 — Use Remind Later

Ask:

> Imagine you cannot do this now but may want to do it later.

Observe:

* Does the user find Remind Later?
* Do they understand what will happen?
* Do they want control over reminder timing?

### Task 9 — Skip a mission

Ask:

> Imagine you do not want to do this mission today.

Observe:

* Does skipping feel safe?
* Does the app shame the user?
* Does Ringo’s response feel supportive?

### Task 10 — React to rewards and streaks

Show the reward moment, XP, streak, or progress feedback.

Ask:

> What do you think this means?

Observe:

* Does the user understand XP?
* Does streak feedback motivate or pressure them?
* Is the reward sequence too slow or satisfying?

### Task 11 — Reminder copy reaction

Show 3–5 example reminder messages.

Ask:

> Which reminder feels most helpful? Which feels annoying or pressuring?

Observe:

* Which wording feels caring?
* Which wording feels guilt-inducing?
* Does Ringo’s personality improve or worsen reminders?

---

## Version B tasks

In the 7–14 day pilot, users should not be forced into artificial tasks every day. They should use the product naturally.

However, the study should encourage these natural actions:

* complete a daily mission when possible
* choose a tiny mission when the normal mission feels too hard
* use “Make it smaller” if needed
* use “I’m too tired” if accurate
* use Remind Later when busy
* skip honestly when they do not want to continue
* respond naturally to reminders
* view reward moments after completion
* continue or stop naturally

The most important instruction is:

> Do not perform for the study. Use the app honestly.

# 9. What Data to Collect

## Data types

Collect four types of data:

1. Usability observation data
2. Behavioral log data
3. Survey data
4. Interview data

Do not collect unnecessary personal or sensitive data.

---

## Version A data collection

### Usability observation data

Record:

* task success or failure
* time to complete each task
* confusion points
* misclicks
* questions asked
* comments during think-aloud
* places where the user needs help
* emotional reactions to Ringo
* emotional reactions to rewards
* emotional reactions to reminders

### Survey data

Collect ratings after the session.

Use a 1–5 scale:

1 = strongly disagree
2 = disagree
3 = neutral
4 = agree
5 = strongly agree

### Interview data

Collect short qualitative responses about:

* what was clear
* what was confusing
* whether Ringo felt supportive
* whether anything felt annoying
* whether tiny missions felt useful
* whether rewards felt good or too much
* whether reminders felt helpful or intrusive
* whether anything created guilt or pressure

---

## Version B data collection

### Behavioral log data

Collect logs for:

* onboarding completed
* path selected
* challenge selected
* daily mission shown
* daily mission completed
* tiny mission shown
* tiny mission selected
* tiny mission completed
* bonus mission shown
* bonus mission selected
* bonus mission completed
* Make it smaller clicked
* I’m too tired clicked
* Remind later clicked
* reminder sent
* reminder opened or clicked
* skip clicked
* reward sequence started
* reward sequence completed
* reward sequence exited early
* streak continued
* streak lost
* user returned next day
* inactive days

### Daily micro-feedback

Ask 2–4 short questions per day.

Example:

1. Did today’s mission feel clear?
2. Did today’s mission feel doable?
3. Did Ringo’s message feel supportive?
4. Did anything feel annoying, pressuring, or guilt-inducing?

Keep this very short. If the daily survey is too long, users will stop answering.

### Exit survey

At the end of the pilot, ask the full survey.

### Follow-up interviews

Interview at least 3–5 users.

If the sample is small, interview all participants if possible.

# 10. Behavioral Metrics

Behavioral metrics should be treated as early engagement and usability indicators.

They are not habit-formation evidence.

## Core metrics

| Metric                        | Meaning                                          | What it can suggest                           | What it cannot prove                 |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Onboarding completion rate    | Users who finish onboarding                      | Whether onboarding is too hard or too long    | Long-term engagement                 |
| Time to first mission         | Time until user reaches first mission            | Initial clarity and friction                  | Motivation or habit strength         |
| First mission completion rate | Users who complete first mission                 | First-session usability                       | Habit formation                      |
| Daily mission completion rate | Missions completed / missions shown              | Short-term engagement                         | Long-term behavior change            |
| Tiny mission selection rate   | Tiny missions selected / tiny missions offered   | Whether users use low-friction alternatives   | That tiny missions improve habits    |
| Tiny mission completion rate  | Tiny missions completed / tiny missions selected | Whether tiny missions feel doable             | That tiny missions improve retention |
| Make it smaller usage         | Clicks on Make it smaller                        | Whether users need lower difficulty           | That adaptation works                |
| I’m too tired usage           | Clicks on tiredness signal                       | Whether users use explicit low-energy input   | Actual emotional state accuracy      |
| Skip rate                     | Skips / missions shown                           | Possible friction, lack of interest, poor fit | Failure or lack of discipline        |
| Remind later rate             | Remind later clicks / missions shown             | Whether postponement is useful                | Future completion                    |
| Reminder response rate        | Reminder clicks or returns / reminders sent      | Basic reminder usefulness                     | That reminders are caring            |
| Reward sequence completion    | Finished reward sequence / started sequence      | Whether reward moment is tolerable            | Reward effectiveness                 |
| Return rate                   | Users returning next day or during pilot         | Short-term engagement                         | Habit formation                      |
| Drop-off point                | Last meaningful action before inactivity         | Where friction may occur                      | Cause of drop-off without interview  |

## Important interpretation warning

Do not interpret skipped missions as laziness.

A skipped mission may mean:

* the mission was unclear
* the mission was too difficult
* the user was busy
* the user was tired
* the user disliked the mission
* the user disliked the app
* the reminder came at a bad time
* the path/challenge was not personally relevant
* the user forgot
* the user had technical problems

Behavior logs should be combined with user feedback.

# 13. Success Criteria

Success criteria should be modest.

The study should not define success as “users formed habits.”

Success should mean:

* users understood the core idea
* users could complete core tasks
* users found the app promising
* major risks were identified
* product improvements became clear

---

## Version A success criteria

For 5–8 participants, success may mean:

* Most participants understand what RingoStrike is after the first session.
* Most participants can find today’s mission without help.
* Most participants can complete a mission without help.
* Most participants understand tiny missions after seeing them.
* Most participants understand “Make it smaller.”
* Most participants understand “I’m too tired.”
* Most participants can explain what Ringo does.
* No more than 1–2 participants report strong guilt, pressure, or manipulation.
* At least 3 major usability issues are identified and converted into product improvements.
* The study produces clear quotes and observations for the paper.

## Version A failure signals

Version A should be treated as a warning if:

* users cannot explain what the app is
* users do not understand paths/challenges/missions
* users cannot find the main mission
* tiny missions feel meaningless
* Ringo feels annoying or childish to most participants
* rewards feel excessive or confusing
* users feel guilt from streaks or reminders
* the app requires too much explanation from the moderator

Failure is useful at this stage. It means the study worked.

---

## Version B success criteria

For a 7–14 day pilot, success may mean:

* most users complete onboarding
* at least half of users return after the first day
* users complete at least some missions without researcher support
* tiny missions are used naturally by some users
* remind-later and skip behavior produce useful design insights
* reminders receive some responses without strong annoyance
* users can describe what Ringo adds to the experience
* users report more support than pressure
* the pilot identifies clear improvements for the next version

## Version B failure signals

Version B should be treated as a warning if:

* users drop off immediately after onboarding
* users ignore reminders completely
* users do not understand what mission to do
* users do not use tiny missions
* users feel judged by Ringo
* streaks create guilt
* reward moments become repetitive after a few days
* users say the app is too complex for daily use
* users cannot explain why they would continue using it

Again, this is not failure of the paper. It is useful formative evidence.

# 14. Risks and Ethical Considerations

## Risk 1 — Pressure and guilt

RingoStrike uses missions, streaks, reminders, and rewards. These can create pressure.

The study must specifically ask whether users feel:

* judged
* guilty
* pressured
* manipulated
* disappointed in themselves
* responsible for making Ringo sad

The app should avoid shame-based messages.

## Risk 2 — Emotional overreach

Ringo is an emotionally supportive companion, but it must not act like a therapist.

Avoid messages that imply:

* clinical support
* mental health treatment
* diagnosis
* emotional dependency
* guaranteed personal improvement

Safe tone:

> “Let’s make it smaller today.”

Unsafe tone:

> “You need me to stay disciplined.”

## Risk 3 — False habit claims

The study must not tell users that the app will build habits.

Use:

> “This app is designed to support small daily actions.”

Avoid:

> “This app will make you disciplined.”

## Risk 4 — Reminder fatigue

Reminders can become annoying or intrusive.

Participants should be able to:

* turn reminders off
* reduce reminder frequency
* choose reminder timing if possible
* stop the study at any time

## Risk 5 — Privacy and behavior logs

The study may collect behavior data.

Participants should be told:

* what data is collected
* why it is collected
* who can see it
* how long it will be stored
* whether it will be anonymized
* how they can request deletion

Do not collect unnecessary sensitive data.

## Risk 6 — Sensitive user states

Do not ask participants to disclose mental health diagnoses, trauma, or medical conditions.

If a user says they are in serious distress, the app should not attempt to handle that as a research feature.

## Risk 7 — Over-personalization

If the app uses adaptive messages, explain whether they are rule-based or AI-assisted.

Do not imply that Ringo deeply understands the user if the system only uses simple behavior signals.

Safe wording:

> “RingoStrike uses selected behavior signals to adjust mission suggestions.”

Avoid:

> “Ringo understands your emotional state.”

## Risk 8 — Testing with friends

Testing with friends can be useful for early UX, but it creates bias.

Friends may:

* be too positive
* avoid criticism
* already understand the project
* want to support the creator

If using friends, describe the sample honestly as convenience sampling.

---

# 15. Limitations

## Version A limitations

Version A has major limitations:

* very small sample
* short session
* artificial testing context
* no real daily use
* possible moderator influence
* possible friend/convenience sample bias
* cannot measure retention
* cannot measure habit formation
* cannot evaluate reminders over time
* cannot evaluate whether rewards become boring
* cannot evaluate whether Ringo remains supportive after repeated use

Version A is useful for finding usability and perception problems.

It is not useful for proving product effectiveness.

## Version B limitations

Version B is stronger, but still limited:

* short duration
* small sample
* no control group
* no randomization
* no validated habit-strength measurement
* novelty effect likely
* users may behave differently because they know they are being studied
* reminders may perform differently over longer periods
* companion effect is not isolated from other features
* behavior logs cannot fully explain user motivation
* engagement is not the same as habit formation
* completion is not the same as wellbeing improvement

Version B can support a formative case study.

It cannot support claims of long-term habit change.

# Recommended Study Order

The recommended order is:

1. Run Version A with 5–8 users.
2. Fix the biggest usability and emotional-risk issues.
3. Improve onboarding, mission center, tiny missions, reminders, and reward flow.
4. Run Version B with 10–20 users for 7–14 days.
5. Use Version B findings as formative pilot evidence in the paper.
6. Only after that, consider a larger comparative or longer-term study.

Do not jump directly into a long pilot if the first-session experience is still unclear.
