# Feature-to-Theory Map — RingoStrike

## Purpose of This File

This file maps major RingoStrike product features to the behavior, motivation, UX, gamification, adaptive intervention, companion-agent, reminder, and ethical design concepts identified in the current research files.

This is a **design rationale map**, not a validation document.

RingoStrike is currently framed as:

> An evidence-informed design framework and early product case study, not a validated habit-change intervention.

The literature may support the **plausibility** of these design decisions, but it does **not** prove that RingoStrike has already improved long-term habit formation, retention, wellbeing, productivity, emotional support, or motivation.

# 1. Paths

## Feature name

Paths

## Short feature description

Paths are broad life-improvement directions such as focus, health, creativity, personal growth, or recovery. They organize the user’s long-term intention into a clearer thematic direction.

## Related theory or concept

* Habit formation and repeated behavior in meaningful contexts
* Goal setting and action planning
* Behavior Change Techniques
* Guided flow and reduced decision burden
* UX simplification

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Lally et al. (2010)
* Wood & Neal (2007)
* Zhu et al. (2024)
* Michie et al. (2013)
* Michie et al. (2011)
* Perski et al. (2017)
* Hentati et al. (2021)

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 6 — Guided flow and reduced confusion
* Claim 7 — Behavior signals as inputs for adaptive support

## Intended behavioral purpose

Paths are intended to help users understand where they are going before selecting specific challenges or missions. They reduce the feeling of facing a random task list by placing daily actions inside a larger direction.

## Possible UX benefit

Paths may make the app feel more structured and easier to understand. They may also support a sense of identity or personal direction, especially when users can choose a path that matches their current goals.

## Risk or limitation

Paths can create complexity if there are too many options or if users do not understand the difference between paths, challenges, and missions. A path system may also make the app feel heavier than necessary for users who only want one simple daily action.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs usability testing to evaluate:

* Whether users understand what a path is.
* Whether path selection feels motivating or confusing.
* Whether users can easily move from path selection to a concrete mission.
* Whether paths reduce or increase decision burden.
* Whether some users prefer skipping paths and starting directly with one mission.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Paths are designed to organize daily habit-building actions into broader self-improvement directions. Their value in reducing confusion or increasing motivation should be evaluated through usability testing and user feedback.

# 2. Challenges

## Feature name

Challenges

## Short feature description

Challenges are structured programs inside each path. They break a broad direction into a more focused sequence of actions or missions.

## Related theory or concept

* Goal setting
* Graded tasks
* Action planning
* Competence support

* Sailer et al. (2017)

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 6 — Guided flow and reduced confusion
* Claim 8 — Optional higher-intensity actions and adaptive difficulty

## Intended behavioral purpose

Challenges are intended to make larger goals feel more manageable by creating a structured progression between broad paths and daily missions.

## Possible UX benefit

Challenges may help users feel that they are following a meaningful program instead of completing disconnected tasks. They can also create a sense of progress and competence when the user moves through stages.

## Risk or limitation

Challenges may become too rigid if users feel forced into a fixed sequence. They may also create pressure if users interpret challenge completion as success or failure rather than flexible progress.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs data showing:

* Whether users understand the relationship between paths, challenges, and missions.
* Whether challenges make progress feel clearer.
* Whether challenge structure increases completion or creates pressure.
* Whether users prefer fixed challenge sequences or flexible mission choice.
* Whether challenge difficulty is appropriate for different user states.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Challenges are designed as structured programs that translate broad goals into smaller, staged actions. Their effect on clarity, motivation, and perceived pressure remains to be tested with RingoStrike users.

# 3. Daily Missions

## Feature name

Daily missions

## Short feature description

Daily missions are the main actions users are asked to complete each day. They represent the normal expected level of effort for a given challenge or path.

## Related theory or concept

* Habit formation
* Repetition
* Context cues

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 7 — Behavior signals as inputs for adaptive support

## Intended behavioral purpose

Daily missions are intended to turn abstract goals into concrete, repeatable actions. They give the user a clear answer to the question: “What should I do today?”

## Possible UX benefit

Daily missions may reduce ambiguity and help users start more easily. A clear daily mission can also make the experience feel focused rather than overwhelming.

## Risk or limitation

Daily missions can become repetitive, boring, too difficult, or poorly matched to the user’s daily state. If the user misses several daily missions, the feature may create guilt or avoidance.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether users understand daily missions clearly.
* Whether users complete them consistently.
* Whether mission difficulty is appropriate.
* Whether missions feel meaningful rather than random.
* Whether repetition supports engagement or becomes boring.
* Whether daily missions happen in stable or meaningful user contexts.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Daily missions are designed to translate long-term goals into concrete, repeatable daily actions. The literature supports the design rationale for repeated action and feedback, but RingoStrike-specific data is needed to evaluate whether daily missions support sustained use or habit formation.

# 4. Tiny Missions

## Feature name

Tiny missions

## Short feature description

Tiny missions are smaller, lower-effort alternatives to normal missions. They are intended for users who feel tired, overwhelmed, inactive, or low on motivation.

## Related theory or concept

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 1 — Tiny missions and friction reduction

## Intended behavioral purpose

Tiny missions are intended to reduce task difficulty during low-energy moments so users can still take a small action instead of fully dropping off.

## Possible UX benefit

## Risk or limitation

Tiny missions may feel too small, childish, or meaningless if not framed well. They may also create false confidence if the paper implies that tiny missions automatically lead to habit formation.

* Whether tiny missions reduce skipping.
* Whether tiny missions increase same-day completion.
* Whether tiny missions help users return after low-energy days.
* Whether users perceive tiny missions as supportive.
* Whether tiny missions are too easy to feel meaningful.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Tiny missions are designed to reduce friction during low-energy moments. Their effect on completion, continuation, retention, or habit formation in RingoStrike requires user testing and behavioral logs.

# 6. Make It Smaller

## Feature name

Make it smaller

## Short feature description

“Make it smaller” is an interaction that lets users reduce the difficulty or size of a mission instead of skipping it entirely.

## Related theory or concept

* Friction reduction
* Graded tasks
* Adaptive mission intensity
* Behavior-aware support
* Autonomy support
* Recovery-oriented design

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Fogg (2009)

* Nahum-Shani et al. (2018)
* Deci & Ryan (2000)

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 1 — Tiny missions and friction reduction
* Claim 2 — Adaptive mission intensity

## Intended behavioral purpose

This feature is intended to preserve action when the original mission feels too difficult. Instead of treating difficulty as failure, the app offers a smaller version of the task.

## Possible UX benefit

“Make it smaller” may make the product feel more flexible and forgiving. It gives users control and may reduce all-or-nothing thinking.

## Risk or limitation

If overused, users may always reduce tasks without progressing. If poorly explained, users may interpret it as lowering standards rather than adapting support. It may also fail if the smaller mission still feels irrelevant or unclear.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether users understand this interaction.
* Whether users use it instead of skipping.
* Whether smaller missions still feel meaningful.
* Whether it reduces perceived pressure.
* Whether it improves completion or return behavior.
* Whether it should be suggested proactively or only shown on request.

## Safe wording for the paper

> “Make it smaller” is designed as a low-friction adaptation pattern that helps users reduce task difficulty instead of abandoning the mission. Its behavioral effect in RingoStrike remains to be evaluated.

# 7. I’m Too Tired

## Feature name

I’m too tired

## Short feature description

“I’m too tired” is a user signal that communicates low energy. The system can respond by offering a tiny mission, recovery mission, supportive message, or reminder option.

## Related theory or concept

* User state signal
* Adaptive intervention tailoring variable
* Autonomy-supportive feedback
* Emotional support
* Friction reduction
* Recovery-oriented behavior design

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Nahum-Shani et al. (2018)
* Klasnja et al. (2015)
* Fogg (2009)
* Baumel & Muench (2021)
* Deci & Ryan (2000)
* Ng et al. (2012)
* Laranjo et al. (2018)

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 1 — Tiny missions and friction reduction
* Claim 2 — Adaptive mission intensity
* Claim 3 — Emotionally supportive companion messages
* Claim 7 — Behavior signals as inputs for adaptive support

## Intended behavioral purpose

This feature is intended to treat tiredness as useful context rather than failure. It gives the system a direct signal that the user may need a lower-pressure action or emotional support.

## Possible UX benefit

“I’m too tired” may help users feel understood and reduce shame around low-energy days. It may also improve adaptation because the system receives explicit user input rather than only inferring state from behavior logs.

## Risk or limitation

The system may misinterpret tiredness or respond with the wrong level of support. If used too often, it may normalize avoidance rather than recovery. It must also avoid sounding therapeutic unless properly validated.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether users are comfortable pressing “I’m too tired.”
* Whether the response feels supportive.
* Whether users prefer a tiny mission, rest option, or reminder after this signal.
* Whether tiredness predicts lower completion.
* Whether this feature reduces guilt or increases avoidance.
* Whether different users interpret the phrase differently.

## Safe wording for the paper

> “I’m too tired” is designed as an explicit low-energy signal that allows RingoStrike to offer lower-pressure support. Future research should test whether users experience this response as supportive, useful, or potentially avoidant.

## Feature name

Mission intensity

## Short feature description

Mission intensity refers to different levels or variants of a mission, such as tiny, normal, bonus, recovery, or low-pressure versions.

## Related theory or concept

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 2 — Adaptive mission intensity
* Claim 7 — Behavior signals as inputs for adaptive support
* Claim 8 — Bonus missions

## Intended behavioral purpose

Mission intensity is intended to match task difficulty to user state, behavior patterns, or motivation level, instead of presenting the same task to every user in every context.

## Possible UX benefit

Adaptive intensity may make the app feel more responsive and less rigid. It may also support both tired users and motivated users without forcing one fixed difficulty level.

## Risk or limitation

Mission intensity can become confusing if users do not understand why a mission changed. It can also overclaim personalization if the system is only rule-based. Poorly designed adaptation may feel random, unfair, or patronizing.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether users understand mission intensity labels.
* Whether intensity changes match user needs.
* Whether rule-based adaptation performs better than static missions.
* Whether users feel helped or misread by adaptation.
* Whether mission intensity affects completion, skipping, or return rate.
* Whether the system should explain why a mission was adapted.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Mission intensity is a behavior-aware design mechanism that offers different task levels based on user signals. RingoStrike currently treats this as a testable adaptation strategy, not as proven personalization.

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether logged signals accurately reflect user state.
* Whether skip, remind later, and inactivity need follow-up questions.
* Whether logs predict useful adaptation.
* Whether users understand what data is collected.
* Whether users consent to behavior tracking.
* Whether logs can support future research without overclaiming.
* Whether privacy and data minimization rules are clear.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Mission logs provide behavior signals that can inform feedback, adaptation, and future evaluation. These signals should be treated as imperfect indicators of user state and must be handled with transparent ethical boundaries.

# 17. Onboarding and Guided Flow

## Feature name

Onboarding and guided flow

## Short feature description

Onboarding and guided flow introduce the user to RingoStrike and guide them toward the next relevant action instead of expecting them to manually explore every path, challenge, and mission.

## Related theory or concept

* UX friction reduction
* Cognitive load
* Guided engagement
* Digital intervention attrition
* Effort-optimized design
* Companion-guided interaction

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Eysenbach (2005)
* Perski et al. (2017)
* Hentati et al. (2021)
* Baumel & Muench (2021)
* Webb et al. (2010)
* Michie et al. (2011)
* Bickmore et al. (2010)

## Related claim number from claims-to-support.md

* Claim 6 — Guided flow and reduced confusion
* Claim 3 — Emotionally supportive companion messages

## Intended behavioral purpose

Onboarding and guided flow are intended to reduce confusion, help users understand the app quickly, and move users toward one clear next action.

## Possible UX benefit

A guided flow may make RingoStrike feel simpler, more supportive, and less overwhelming. It may also align with the goal that users should not need to search for what to do next.

## Risk or limitation

A guided flow can feel restrictive if users want freedom. Onboarding can also become too long, too cute, or too instructional. If users still feel confused after onboarding, the feature has failed its purpose.

## What evidence is still needed

RingoStrike needs evidence showing:

* Whether users understand the app after onboarding.
* Whether users know what to do next.
* Whether guided flow reduces confusion.
* Whether users prefer Ringo-led guidance or manual navigation.
* Whether onboarding is too long or too short.
* Whether users understand paths, challenges, missions, tiny missions, and rewards.
* Whether the simplified dashboard improves first-session completion.

## Safe wording for the paper

> Onboarding and guided flow are designed to reduce decision burden and clarify the next action. Their effectiveness should be evaluated through usability testing focused on clarity, friction, and user confidence.

# Cross-Feature Design Risks

## 1. Overclaiming effectiveness

The largest risk is writing as if these features are already proven to work in RingoStrike.

Avoid:

> RingoStrike improves habit formation.

Use:

> RingoStrike translates habit-formation and behavior-change concepts into a testable design framework.

---

## 2. Confusing engagement with habit formation

Mission completion, streaks, XP, and return rate may show engagement, but they do not automatically prove habit formation.

Future habit-related evidence would require longer-term data, repeated behavior in meaningful contexts, and ideally validated habit-strength measures.

---

## 3. Treating rule-based adaptation as AI personalization

If RingoStrike currently uses rule-based adaptation, the paper should say so clearly.

Safe wording:

> RingoStrike currently explores behavior-aware or rule-based adaptation and may later be extended with AI-assisted personalization.

Avoid:

> RingoStrike uses AI to personalize habit formation.

unless that system is actually implemented and evaluated.

---

## 4. Ignoring emotional and ethical risks

Ringo’s supportive personality is a strength, but companion systems can also create guilt, pressure, dependency, false intimacy, or manipulative persuasion.

The paper should explicitly discuss boundaries around:

* Shame-based messages
* Streak pressure
* Reminder fatigue
* Data use
* Over-personalization
* Emotional dependency
* Therapeutic or medical claims

---

## 5. Making the system too complex

Paths, challenges, missions, tiny missions, bonus missions, XP, streaks, reminders, moods, and reward moments can make the product rich, but they can also overload users.

The first user study should specifically test:

* Whether users understand the hierarchy.
* Whether they know what to do next.
* Whether Ringo reduces or adds complexity.
* Whether the mission center feels simple enough.
* Whether reward moments are satisfying or excessive.

# Paper-Level Safe Summary

RingoStrike’s features can be presented as a structured attempt to translate habit formation, behavior-change techniques, adaptive intervention design, motivation theory, gamification, companion-agent research, reminder design, UX engagement research, and ethical persuasive design into a practical habit-building interface.

However, this map should be used only as a **design rationale**.

The paper should not claim that RingoStrike has proven long-term habit change, improved retention, improved wellbeing, or validated the effectiveness of tiny missions, reminders, companion messages, streaks, or rewards.

The strongest defensible framing is:

> RingoStrike is an evidence-informed design framework and early case study that proposes adaptive micro-missions and emotionally supportive companion interaction as testable design strategies for habit-building apps.

Future work should evaluate these strategies through usability testing, behavioral logs, user interviews, reminder-response data, and longer-term habit-related measurement.
