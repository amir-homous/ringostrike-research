# Claims to Support

This file tracks the main claims that may appear in the RingoStrike paper and the evidence needed to support them.

The claim system below uses the **official 10 claims** from `literature-review-plan.md`.

The older provisional literature-matrix clusters such as `C1`, `C2`, `C3`, etc. were used only as a temporary source-mapping system. They should **not** be used as the final claim structure.

## Evidence Position

RingoStrike is currently framed as:

> An evidence-informed design framework and early product case study, not a validated habit-change intervention.

The literature may support the **design rationale** behind RingoStrike features, but it does **not** prove that RingoStrike has already produced long-term habit change, improved wellbeing, increased retention, or changed user behavior in a scientifically validated way.

RingoStrike-specific claims require future user data, such as:

* Mission completion logs
* Tiny mission usage
* Skip and remind-later data
* Reminder response data
* Retention and return-rate data
* User interviews
* Usability testing
* Perceived support and pressure surveys
* Longer-term habit-strength measurement

# Claim 1

## Original claim wording

Tiny missions may reduce friction when users feel tired, overwhelmed, or low on motivation.

## Safer academic wording

Tiny missions are designed to reduce task difficulty and perceived effort during low-energy moments. Prior behavior-design and digital-intervention literature supports the plausibility of reducing friction, but RingoStrike-specific evidence is needed to determine whether tiny missions improve completion, continuation, or retention.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Tiny missions
* “Make it smaller”
* “I’m too tired”
* Recovery missions
* Mission intensity
* Low-pressure alternatives

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Fogg (2009) — behavior model for persuasive design
* Baumel & Muench (2021) — effort-optimized intervention model
* Michie et al. (2011) — Behaviour Change Wheel
* Michie et al. (2013) — Behavior Change Technique Taxonomy
* Zhu et al. (2024) — digital behavior change intervention designs for habit formation

## Evidence strength

Moderate conceptual support.

The strongest support is theoretical and framework-based. The literature supports the design logic of reducing effort and making actions easier to start, but does not directly prove that RingoStrike tiny missions work.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Easier actions may require less motivation to begin.
* Digital interventions should reduce unnecessary user effort.
* Behavior change systems should consider capability, opportunity, and motivation.
* Small, structured actions can be part of behavior-change design.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether users choose tiny missions when tired or overwhelmed.
* Whether tiny missions reduce skipping.
* Whether tiny missions increase same-day completion.
* Whether tiny missions help users return after low-energy days.
* Whether users perceive tiny missions as supportive rather than childish or too easy.

## Current status

Cautious.

## Overclaiming risk

High.

Avoid saying:

> Tiny missions help users build habits.

Better wording:

> Tiny missions are a design strategy intended to reduce friction during low-energy moments. Their effect in RingoStrike requires user testing and behavioral logs.

# Claim 2

## Original claim wording

Adaptive mission intensity may help users continue after skipping, postponing, or showing low energy.

## Safer academic wording

Adaptive mission intensity is designed to provide different levels of task difficulty and support based on user behavior signals such as skipping, postponing, tiredness, or inactivity. JITAI and adaptive intervention literature supports the design rationale for tailoring support, but RingoStrike-specific data is required to evaluate whether this adaptation improves continuation.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Adaptive mission intensity
* Tiny / normal / bonus mission variants
* Remind later
* Skip interpretation
* Recovery missions
* Tiredness signal
* Streak risk detection
* Adaptive Ringo messages

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Nahum-Shani et al. (2018) — JITAI design principles
* Klasnja et al. (2015) — microrandomized trials for adaptive interventions
* Mehrotra et al. (2016) — notification receptivity and context
* Alslaity et al. (2023) — personalization in persuasive and behavior change interventions

## Evidence strength

Moderate to strong for the adaptive-intervention design rationale.

Weak to absent for RingoStrike-specific effectiveness.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Adaptive interventions can use decision points, tailoring variables, intervention options, and decision rules.
* Timing, context, and receptivity matter when delivering support.
* Adaptive systems should be evaluated carefully rather than assumed to work.
* Personalization is promising but context-dependent.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether adaptive mission intensity reduces drop-off.
* Whether users accept tiny/recovery missions after skipping or postponing.
* Whether adaptive messages improve continuation.
* Whether rule-based adaptation performs better than static missions.
* Whether users understand why the mission changed.

## Current status

Cautious.

## Overclaiming risk

Very high.

Avoid saying:

> RingoStrike adapts missions to keep users engaged.

Better wording:

> RingoStrike explores rule-based behavior-aware adaptation. Its effect on engagement or continuation requires future evaluation.

# Claim 3

## Original claim wording

Emotionally supportive companion messages may make habit-building feel less judgmental and more approachable.

## Safer academic wording

Emotionally supportive companion messages are intended to make RingoStrike feel less mechanical, less judgmental, and more approachable. Literature on Self-Determination Theory, conversational agents, and relational agents supports the plausibility of supportive tone and perceived relatedness, but user perception data is needed to determine how users actually experience Ringo.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Ringo companion
* Ringo moods
* Supportive messages
* Recovery messages
* Encouragement after skipping
* Celebration after completion
* Guided onboarding
* Adaptive emotional feedback

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Deci & Ryan (2000) — Self-Determination Theory
* Ng et al. (2012) — SDT in health contexts
* Laranjo et al. (2018) — conversational agents in healthcare
* Bickmore et al. (2010) — relational agents and long-term engagement
* Sailer et al. (2017) — gamification elements and psychological need satisfaction

## Evidence strength

Moderate.

There is stronger support for autonomy-supportive communication and relatedness as motivational concepts than for the specific claim that Ringo improves habit-building experience.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Autonomy, competence, and relatedness are important motivational needs.
* Supportive, non-controlling feedback may be better than pressure-based feedback.
* Relational or conversational agents can be designed to support repeated engagement.
* Companion-like interfaces may influence perceived support, trust, and emotional connection.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether users perceive Ringo as supportive.
* Whether users find Ringo’s tone caring or annoying.
* Whether Ringo reduces guilt after missed missions.
* Whether Ringo improves clarity or motivation.
* Whether different users respond differently to companion language.

## Current status

Cautious.

## Overclaiming risk

High.

Avoid saying:

> Ringo makes users feel supported.

Better wording:

> Ringo is designed to provide emotionally supportive feedback. Whether users experience it that way should be evaluated through interviews, surveys, and usability testing.

# Claim 4

## Original claim wording

Streaks, rewards, and progress feedback may support engagement, but may also create pressure or guilt.

## Safer academic wording

Streaks, rewards, and progress feedback can be designed as engagement and feedback mechanisms, but their effects are context-dependent. Gamification and motivation literature suggests that progress feedback may support engagement, while controlling rewards or loss-based streak pressure may create guilt, pressure, or reduced autonomy.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Streaks
* XP
* Rewards
* Achievements
* Progress bars
* Reward moments
* Completion celebration
* Streak risk warnings

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Hamari et al. (2014) — gamification literature review
* Sailer et al. (2017) — effects of game design elements on psychological need satisfaction
* Harkin et al. (2016) — progress monitoring and goal attainment
* Deci et al. (1999) — extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation
* Deci & Ryan (2000) — Self-Determination Theory

## Evidence strength

Mixed but useful.

Strong support exists for progress monitoring as a general goal-support mechanism. Gamification evidence is more context-dependent. Reward-related literature also warns that some extrinsic rewards may undermine intrinsic motivation if they feel controlling.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Progress monitoring can support goal pursuit.
* Game elements may support engagement when designed carefully.
* Different gamification mechanics affect motivation differently.
* Rewards can become harmful if they feel controlling.
* Streaks and rewards should be framed as feedback, not moral judgment.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether users feel motivated by streaks and XP.
* Whether users feel pressure, guilt, or anxiety from streak loss.
* Whether reward moments feel satisfying or excessive.
* Whether rewards increase meaningful engagement or only short-term checking.
* Whether streak warnings should be softened, optional, or personalized.

## Current status

Cautious.

## Overclaiming risk

Very high.

Avoid saying:

> Streaks and rewards improve habit formation.

Better wording:

> Streaks, rewards, and progress feedback may support engagement when designed carefully, but they also carry risks of pressure and guilt.

## Safer academic wording

Ringo-style reminders are designed to feel more supportive and less mechanical by using companion-based, autonomy-respecting language. Reminder, notification, SDT, and conversational-agent literature supports the importance of timing, tone, and user receptivity, but RingoStrike-specific feedback is needed to determine whether users actually perceive these reminders as caring.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Daily reminders
* Missed mission reminders
* Streak risk reminders
* Ringo notification copy
* Remind later
* Supportive reminder tone
* Context-aware reminder timing, if added later

* Laranjo et al. (2018) — conversational agents
* Bickmore et al. (2010) — relational agents

## Evidence strength

Moderate.

The literature supports careful reminder design, notification timing, and autonomy-supportive tone. It does not prove that Ringo-style reminders are perceived as more caring.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Reminders can support behavior-change interventions.
* Notification timing and receptivity matter.
* Reminder quality matters, not only reminder frequency.
* Supportive, autonomy-preserving language is theoretically preferable to controlling language.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether users find Ringo reminders caring.
* Whether Ringo reminders reduce annoyance compared with neutral reminders.
* Whether users respond to reminders.
* Whether reminders increase return rate or mission completion.
* Whether reminders create guilt or pressure.

## Current status

Needs user data.

## Overclaiming risk

High.

Avoid saying:

> Ringo reminders are more caring than normal reminders.

Better wording:

> Ringo reminders are designed to use supportive, autonomy-respecting language. User testing is needed to evaluate whether they are perceived as caring rather than intrusive.

* Michie et al. (2011) — Behaviour Change Wheel

Moderate.

The literature supports reducing effort, measuring engagement, and treating interface design as important. It does not directly prove that the RingoStrike guided flow is clearer.

The literature supports the idea that:

* Engagement includes both behavior and subjective experience.

## Current status

Needs user data.

Medium to high.

> The guided flow is designed to reduce confusion and decision burden. This should be evaluated through usability testing.

Behavior signals such as completion, skipping, postponing, and inactivity can be useful inputs for adaptive support.

## Safer academic wording

Behavior signals such as completion, skipping, postponing, and inactivity can be treated as potential tailoring variables for adaptive support. Adaptive intervention literature supports the use of decision points, tailoring variables, and decision rules, but RingoStrike must validate whether these signals accurately represent user needs and lead to helpful adaptations.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Mission logs
* Completion state
* Skip
* Remind later
* Inactivity tracking
* Streak risk
* Adaptive Ringo messages
* Adaptive mission intensity
* Recovery missions

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Nahum-Shani et al. (2018) — JITAI components and design principles
* Klasnja et al. (2015) — microrandomized trials
* Mehrotra et al. (2016) — notification receptivity and contextual factors
* Perski et al. (2017) — engagement with digital behavior change interventions
* Alslaity et al. (2023) — personalization in persuasive systems

## Evidence strength

Strong as a design principle.

Weak as a product-specific claim until RingoStrike logs are analyzed.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Adaptive support can be based on user state, context, timing, or behavior.
* Decision rules should be explicit and testable.
* Receptivity varies by person, context, and moment.
* Engagement should be measured as both behavior and experience.
* Personalization requires caution and validation.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence showing:

* Whether skip, postpone, and inactivity reliably indicate low energy, overload, confusion, or lack of interest.
* Whether completion patterns predict useful mission intensity changes.
* Whether adaptive messages improve user experience.
* Whether users feel understood or misread by the system.
* Whether behavior signals should be combined with direct self-report.

## Current status

Cautious.

## Overclaiming risk

Very high.

Avoid saying:

> RingoStrike understands user behavior.

Better wording:

> RingoStrike uses selected behavior signals as design inputs for adaptive support. Future work should test whether these signals accurately reflect user needs.

# Claim 10

## Original claim wording

An emotionally supportive habit companion must include ethical boundaries to avoid manipulation, guilt, dependency, or over-personalization.

## Safer academic wording

Emotionally supportive companion systems should include ethical boundaries around persuasion, personalization, reminders, emotional tone, and data use. Persuasive technology, digital wellbeing, personalization, and conversational-agent literature supports the need for autonomy, transparency, privacy, safety, and anti-shame design principles.

## Related RingoStrike features

* Ringo messages
* Reminders
* Streak warnings
* Adaptive feedback
* AI-assisted personalization, if added
* User behavior tracking
* Reward systems
* Companion emotional tone
* Consent and transparency

## Supporting sources from literature-matrix.md

* Berdichevsky & Neuenschwander (1999) — ethics of persuasive technology
* Maeckelberghe et al. (2023) — ethical challenges of personalized digital health
* Alslaity et al. (2023) — personalization in persuasive and behavior change interventions
* Laranjo et al. (2018) — conversational agent evidence and safety limitations
* Deci & Ryan (2000) — autonomy-supportive motivation

## Evidence strength

Strong for the ethical design requirement.

The evidence supports the need for ethical boundaries. It does not prove that RingoStrike has already implemented those boundaries effectively.

## What the literature supports

The literature supports the idea that:

* Persuasive systems should avoid unethical persuasion.
* Personalization raises privacy, fairness, autonomy, and accountability concerns.
* Emotional companion systems require safety boundaries.
* AI or adaptive systems should be transparent about what they do.
* Shame, guilt, and manipulative pressure should be avoided.
* User autonomy should be protected.

## What still requires RingoStrike-specific user data

RingoStrike still needs evidence and documentation showing:

* What behavior data is collected.
* How adaptive messages are generated.
* Whether adaptation is rule-based or AI-assisted.
* How users can control reminders.
* Whether users feel pressured or supported.
* Whether companion language creates guilt, dependency, or false intimacy.
* What safety rules guide Ringo’s responses.

## Current status

Safe.

This is safe as an ethical design principle, not as a claim that RingoStrike has already solved the issue.

## Overclaiming risk

Medium.

Avoid saying:

> RingoStrike avoids manipulation and guilt.

Better wording:

> RingoStrike should include explicit ethical boundaries to reduce the risk of manipulation, guilt, dependency, and over-personalization.

# Paper-Level Claim Boundary

The paper may safely say:

> The literature supports the design rationale for RingoStrike’s adaptive micro-missions, supportive feedback, reminders, progress systems, and ethical companion design.

The paper should not say:

> RingoStrike has been proven to create long-term habits.

The strongest current framing is:

> RingoStrike is an evidence-informed design framework and early case study that translates habit formation, behavior change, adaptive intervention, motivation, gamification, companion-agent, reminder, UX, and ethical persuasive design literature into a testable product framework.

Future user research should evaluate whether the design actually improves clarity, perceived support, reduced friction, mission completion, reminder response, and longer-term habit-related outcomes.
